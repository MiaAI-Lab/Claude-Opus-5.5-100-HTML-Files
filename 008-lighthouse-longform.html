Life at the Edge of the Light

By Ada Penrose  ·  Drawings by Tomas Lind

They lived on rocks at the ends of the map, in towers built to be seen and never visited. This is a story about the watching — and about the evening it stopped.

TThe light was never the keeper’s to own. It belonged to ships that would never learn his name, to trawlers running late for harbour, to the sleepless on the mainland who glanced up at two in the morning and found, far out on the black water, a small star turning. The keeper only tended it, the way you tend a fire in a house where everyone else has gone to bed.

I have spent most of a winter trying to imagine what that tending felt like. What follows is not history. It is a composite, a weather report from a country that no longer exists: the country of the last keepers, who lived at the edge of the light and then, one by one, were asked to leave it.

IThe Rock

ReliefSupply boats kept the sea’s timetable, not the calendar’s. A keeper’s larder was a hedge against a fortnight of weather.

Picture a rock with no trees on it. A tower, white banded in red, rising from basalt the colour of wet slate. A cottage with walls a yard thick and windows set deep as eyes. A vegetable patch the gulls regard as common land. A derrick for the supply boat, which comes when the sea allows and not a day before.

Everything on the rock is measured in tides and turns. The keeper knows the hour by the colour of the water and the week by the level of oil in the store. He knows which of the hundred and eleven stairs creaks in a north wind and which one weeps salt in the thaw. He has learned the particular silence that falls between one wave and the next, and learned not to trust it. Darkness was never the enemy. Darkness was the job.

IIThe Watch

At dusk the ritual begins. He climbs with a lamp in one hand and a cloth in the other, and he does what keepers have always done first: he polishes. Salt, soot, the fine grey bloom the fog leaves behind — all of it steals light, and light is the only thing he is here to give away.

Then the wick, trimmed square. Then the flame, coaxed from blue to gold. Then the clockwork, wound by hand, a weight sinking slowly through the heart of the tower so that the great lens can turn on its bath of mercury — tonnes of glass floating like a leaf on a pond.

And then the long part. The watch. The sea goes from pewter to ink. The mainland lights come out like someone sowing seed. Somewhere past midnight the stars lose their argument with the cloud, and the only light left in the world seems to be the one above his head, pouring out in two slow arms, sweeping the water, touching nothing, touching everything.

The logEntries were terse as telegrams: wind, sea, visibility, vessels. A dull page meant a good night.

He writes in the log every four hours: wind, visibility, the state of the sea, vessels observed. Most nights the entries are dull, and the dullness is the achievement. A quiet log means that no one, anywhere within reach of the beam, has come to grief.

IIIThe Lens

Up close the lens is not a single glass eye at all but a beehive of prisms, ring upon ring of them, each set at its own precise angle to catch a sliver of the flame and bend it level. Stand inside it on a clear afternoon and you are in a cathedral made of rainbows. Keepers drew the curtains by day for good reason: glass that can throw a flame across twenty miles of sea can also, turned toward the sun, set fire to the room it lives in.

IVThe Last Light

Then the machines came, as machines do, patient and very reasonable. A sensor to know the dusk. A motor to turn the lens. A bulb that did not need trimming, and a changer that swapped it when it failed. A radio link, so that someone on the mainland, drinking tea in an office, could see that all was well.

AutomationThrough the twentieth century most of the world’s lighthouses lost their keepers; many now run on sunlight and small, patient lamps.

Nobody could argue with it. The light would still burn. The ships would still pass safely. Only the watching would stop — the human part, the part that had never appeared on any chart.

On the last evening our composite keeper — let us give him that much — climbs the stair one more time. He polishes glass that no longer needs him. He writes a final line in the log, and it is only the weather: wind light, sea slight, visibility good. Then he walks down, shuts a door that will not be opened again for months, and stands on the landing stage while the boat comes in.

Behind him, at dusk, exactly on time and entirely alone, the light comes on.

The towers stand yet, most of them. Their lamps have been swapped for smaller, cleverer things, and some of the great lenses have been lifted out and set behind museum glass, where children press their faces close and watch the room turn inside out. If you are ever on a coast at nightfall, watch for the flash. Count the seconds between. Every light has its own rhythm, its own signature written on the dark, and somebody, once, stayed up all night to keep it true.

Notes

  1. The stair count is invented, like the rock itself. Real towers vary enormously in height, and so in the number of steps a keeper climbed each night.
  2. From the late nineteenth century many large rotating lenses floated in a trough of mercury, which let a falling clockwork weight turn several tonnes of glass with very little friction.
  3. Keepers hung linen curtains or blinds inside the lantern during the day, to shield the lens from the sun and to stop it concentrating sunlight into heat.
  4. A light’s pattern of flashes and eclipses is called its characteristic. It is printed beside the tower on nautical charts, so a navigator can tell one light from another in the dark.

A note on the story. “The Last Keepers” is a work of fiction. The rock, the keeper, the writer, the illustrator and Tidewater Quarterly are all imagined. The lens is real.
