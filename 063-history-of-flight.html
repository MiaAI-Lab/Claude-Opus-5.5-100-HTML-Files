  1. The Montgolfier balloon

    21 November 1783 · Château de la Muette, Paris

    A balloon built by the paper-making brothers Joseph-Michel and Jacques-Étienne Montgolfier lifted Jean-François Pilâtre de Rozier and the Marquis d'Arlandes from the grounds of the Château de la Muette. A fire in a brazier slung beneath the opening kept it aloft. It drifted across Paris for about 25 minutes and came down some 9 km away, the first untethered crewed flight in history.

  2. Cayley's glider

    1853 · Brompton Dale, near Scarborough, Yorkshire

    Half a century earlier, Sir George Cayley had set out the principles of the modern aeroplane: separate means of lift, propulsion and control. In 1853 his full-size glider carried an adult across Brompton Dale. Family tradition says the reluctant pilot was Cayley's coachman, who is said to have given notice as soon as he was back on the ground.

  3. Lilienthal's gliders

    1891 · Derwitz, near Potsdam

    In 1891 the Berlin engineer Otto Lilienthal made his first successful glides from a hillside at Derwitz. Over the next five years he made about 2,000 flights in a series of hang gliders, steering by shifting his weight, with glides of up to 250 metres. Photographs of him in the air were printed around the world and inspired, among others, the Wright brothers.

  4. The Wright Flyer

    17 December 1903 · Kill Devil Hills, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina

    Orville Wright flew the Flyer for 12 seconds and 120 feet (37 m) into a freezing headwind while Wilbur ran alongside. The brothers made four flights that day. The longest, with Wilbur at the controls, covered 852 feet in 59 seconds. The engine mattered, but the real breakthrough was their three-axis control, built around wing-warping.

  5. Blériot crosses the Channel

    25 July 1909 · near Calais to Dover

    Early in the morning Louis Blériot took off from near Calais in his Type XI monoplane. He had no compass. About 36½ minutes later he landed in a meadow beside Dover Castle. The crossing won him the Daily Mail's £1,000 prize, and it showed how quickly the aeroplane was going to shrink the map.

  6. Alcock and Brown

    14–15 June 1919 · St John's, Newfoundland to Clifden, Ireland

    John Alcock and Arthur Whitten Brown flew a modified Vickers Vimy bomber through almost 16 hours of fog, sleet and ice. They came down in the west of Ireland and landed nose-down in a bog that they had taken for a meadow. It was the first non-stop crossing of the Atlantic by air.

  7. Lindbergh, alone

    20–21 May 1927 · Roosevelt Field, New York to Le Bourget, Paris

    The Spirit of St. Louis carried so much fuel that a tank sat where the windscreen would have been. Lindbergh looked ahead through a periscope and the side windows. He landed at Le Bourget 33½ hours after leaving Long Island, to a vast crowd, and won the $25,000 Orteig Prize.

  8. Amelia Earhart

    20–21 May 1932 · Harbour Grace, Newfoundland to Culmore, Northern Ireland

    Earhart took off in a red Lockheed Vega 5B and had a rough night. Ice formed on the wings, the altimeter failed and a cracked exhaust manifold spat flames. About 15 hours later she landed in a pasture near Derry. She was the first woman, and the second person, to fly the Atlantic alone.

  9. The first jet

    27 August 1939 · Rostock-Marienehe, Germany

    Test pilot Erich Warsitz flew the Heinkel He 178 from the company's airfield. It was powered by Hans von Ohain's HeS 3B turbojet, and it was the first aircraft to fly on jet power alone. Days later the Second World War began, and jet propulsion went from curiosity to priority.

  10. Through the sound barrier

    14 October 1947 · over the Mojave Desert, California

    Captain Charles "Chuck" Yeager was dropped from the bomb bay of a B-29. He lit the four rocket chambers of the Bell X-1, which he had named Glamorous Glennis, and reached Mach 1.06 more than 40,000 feet up. The feared "barrier" turned out to be a smooth ride once he was through. He flew with two broken ribs that he had kept quiet about.

  11. Sputnik 1

    4 October 1957 · launched from Tyuratam (Baikonur), Kazakhstan

    Sputnik 1 was a 58 cm aluminium sphere with four whip antennas. Radio amateurs around the world picked up its "beep-beep" for 21 days, until the batteries ran out. The satellite burned up in the atmosphere in January 1958, and the space age had begun.

  12. Gagarin orbits the Earth

    12 April 1961 · launched from Baikonur

    The 27-year-old Soviet pilot Yuri Gagarin lifted off aboard Vostok 1 with the word "Poyekhali!" ("Let's go!"). He made a single orbit and re-entered. As planned, he ejected at about 7 km and landed by parachute near Saratov. The whole flight took 108 minutes.

  13. Apollo 11: the Moon

    20 July 1969 · Sea of Tranquility

    The lunar module Eagle set down with Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin aboard while Michael Collins orbited overhead in Columbia. A few hours later Armstrong stepped onto the surface. Just 66 years after twelve seconds at Kitty Hawk, people were walking on another world.
