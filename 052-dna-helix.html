The Double Helix

Two antiparallel strands wound around one axis, held together by paired bases, ten and a half pairs to every turn.

Anatomy

  1. 1

    5′ → 3′ direction

    The strands are antiparallel. The teal strand runs 5′→3′ along the axis; its red partner runs 5′→3′ the opposite way. The small chevrons show each strand's direction.

  2. 2

    Sugar–phosphate backbone

    Each strand's outer rail: deoxyribose sugars and phosphate groups joined by phosphodiester bonds. The phosphates give DNA its negative charge.

  3. 3

    Major groove

    The wide gap, about 2.2 nm across. The edges of the bases are most exposed here, so many DNA-binding proteins read the sequence through it.

  4. 4

    Minor groove

    The narrow gap, about 1.2 nm across. The grooves differ because the two backbones are not directly opposite each other around the axis.

  5. 5

    Hydrogen bonds A=T · 2G≡C · 3

    Adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, and guanine with cytosine through three. A purine always faces a pyrimidine, so every rung is the same width.

The geometry follows idealised B-DNA: 10.5 bp per turn, 0.34 nm rise, about 2 nm across, with the two backbones offset by roughly 135° around the axis. Replication here is simplified: helicase, primase and polymerase are left out, and both new strands are drawn growing in the same direction.
